This is the first issue of what we're planning to make a monthly PDX Hackerspace
update: what happened around the space, what people are building, what's coming
up, and where more hands would be useful. August gave us plenty to start with.

## $96,000 Creative Heights Grant

The Oregon Community Foundation has awarded a $96,000
[Creative Heights grant](https://oregoncf.org/news/creative-heights-grants-champion-oregon-artists){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}
to [The Haptic Petting Zoo](https://dillonsimeone.com/Projects/HapticPettingZoo/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"},
a project led by artist and design engineer
[Dillon Simeone](https://dillonsimeone.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, with PDX Hackerspace as fiscal
sponsor. It's one of the projects in OCF's $1 million 2026 Creative Heights
round.

Dillon's work explores ways of translating information across senses: sound you
can feel through haptics, sound you can see through light, wearable interfaces,
movement, physical feedback, and other ways of making information tangible.

The Haptic Petting Zoo grows out of that work. Earlier iterations have included
vibrating benches, GELU wearable bracers, haptic vests, tactile transducers, and
other experiments in turning sound and information into physical and
multisensory experiences.

<img src="{{ '/images/newsletter/2026-09-haptic-zoo.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Four people wearing haptic vests and lit wearable bracers at a Haptic Zoo demo, one holding a brass circuit board" />

With support from Creative Heights, the Haptic Petting Zoo is expanding into a
10+ station interactive touring exhibition. Dillon's
[press kit](https://dillonsimeone.com/Projects/HapticPettingZoo/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} has the full
announcement, the build tracks, and the technical background.

It isn't one sculpture or one machine. It's a collection of interactive works
exploring different ways people can experience and interact with information
through touch, sound, light, motion, temperature, pressure, and other sensory
channels.

Some are sculptural. Some move. Some vibrate. Some of Dillon's work is wearable.
Some translates sound into light. The common thread is experimentation with
perception, accessibility, and interfaces that go well beyond the usual screen,
speaker, keyboard, and mouse.

That's a pretty natural fit for a hackerspace.

Development and fabrication will take place collaboratively at PDX Hackerspace,
with the exhibition eventually heading out on a two-year regional tour.

We'll have considerably more to share as the individual projects develop.

## We Have a Hacker Bus

August also brought a somewhat harder-to-miss project to the space: a 2000 Ford
Econoline short bus.

<img src="{{ '/images/newsletter/2026-09-hacker-bus.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Left: a yellow 2000 Ford Econoline short bus parked outside. Right: the stripped interior, with a workbench, drawer units, pegboard, and remaining passenger seats" />

We don't have a proper name for it yet, and we haven't decided exactly what it's
going to become. That's intentional.

Right now we're stripping out what we don't need, sorting out the basic
infrastructure, figuring out the interior, and discussing what actually makes
sense to do with a mobile hackerspace platform before committing to a design.

There are plenty of ideas. Some will survive contact with reality.

What we do know is that it's going to be a community build. There's already
mechanical work, electrical work, fabrication, CAD, software, design,
documentation, and a large amount of general problem solving involved.

If you've been looking for an impractically large project to contribute to,
we've solved that problem for you.

Also: the bus needs a name.
[Send us one]({{ site.data.contact.contact_form }}){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, or bring it to a Saturday
work party.

## Hack the Hackerspace

PDX Hackerspace is itself one of the larger projects at PDX Hackerspace.

There is always infrastructure to improve, documentation to write, benches to
organize, software to fix, fixtures to build, things to repair, projects that
stalled halfway through, and old decisions that could benefit from somebody
looking at them again.

You don't need to wait for somebody to assign you a task.

If you see something that could be improved, talk to the people who use it,
figure out what needs to happen, and help make it happen. Saturday work parties
are a particularly good place to start. If you'd rather bring materials than
hours, [what we need]({{ '/what-we-need.html' | relative_url }}) is kept current.

## See You in September

The hackerspace works because people show up.

They bring projects. They teach each other things. They fix broken
infrastructure. They organize events. They donate equipment. They clean up
messes they didn't make. They build things nobody had a particularly good reason
to build in the first place.

August brought a major grant, a very large new project parked outside, and
plenty of work inside the building.

September should be interesting.
